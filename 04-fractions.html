Section 2: Key Conceptual Shifts & Pedagogical Traps

Research & Evidence
Shift 1 (ES1 → S1)

Single Halving to Repeated Halving

Moving from a single cut of a ribbon to successive halving (producing quarters and eighths). Crucially, students learn reversible reasoning: re-creating the entire whole when given a half length or half a collection.

Shift 2 (S1 → S2)

Halving to Thirds, Fifths & Number Lines

Thirds and fifths cannot begin with halving; they require iterative folding and adjusting. Fraction strips are mapped onto number lines like rulers, establishing fractions as single numbers with specific linear positions.

Shift 3 (S2 → S3)

Contextual Whole to Universal Whole '1'

In Stage 2, the whole is the length of the specific ribbon or strip. In Stage 3 quantity fractions, the whole is always the abstract number 1. Fractions represent division (34 is 3 ÷ 4) and operators on quantities.

Pedagogical Traps Highlighted by NESA & Academic Literature

Trap 1: The 'Shading Shapes' Trap & Whole-Number Thinking Gould (2014)

Shading parts inside circles or rectangles encourages students to view fractions as two separate whole numbers (counting shaded parts vs counting total parts). In a landmark Australian study of 52,000 Year 7 students, over half estimated 45 + 1112 as 15 or 17 (Gould 2014), directly operating on numerators or denominators.

• NESA Fix: Introduce the continuous linear model first (fraction strips and number lines) where magnitude is visualised as length from 0 to 1.

Trap 2: Saying "2 over 3" or "2 out of 3" instead of "Two-Thirds" Way & Bobis (2011)

Reading 23 as "two over three" or "two out of three" creates an active cognitive barrier to recognising it as a single distinct number on the number line. "Two out of three" also prevents understanding fractions greater than one (e.g. how do you take "four out of three"?).

• NESA Fix: Strictly say "two-thirds". Denominator names the fraction family (Latin that which names); numerator counts the parts (Latin counter). Use "fraction bar" rather than complex terms like vinculum.

Trap 3: Rushing to Procedural Algorithms without Intuitive Strategies Empson & Levi (2011)

Empson and Levi emphasise that if students lack intuitive strategies for tasks such as 1 − 13 or 2 − 58 (e.g. understanding that 58 is 38 away from 1, so the result is 138), they are not yet cognitively ready for abstract algorithms or finding common denominators.

• NESA Fix: Ground early operations in the complement principle (15 and 45 complete the whole) and tape diagrams.

Section 4: Student Diagnostic Clinic & Root Misconceptions

Clinical Diagnostic Sorter

Mathematics education research identifies that most fraction misunderstandings stem from four core root misconceptions. Review the authentic classroom transcript below, then test your diagnostic skills across 11 authentic student responses.

Authentic Clinical Interview Protocol (Gould 2013 / Ross 2002 Adaptation)

Teacher: "Here is a number line with endpoints 0 and 2. Where would the fraction 14 go?"

Student (Year 4): [Immediately places the peg one-quarter of the way along the total line, right at 0.5.]

Teacher: "Can you tell me how you decided to place it there?"

Student: "Well, one-quarter means you break the whole line into 4 pieces. So it goes at the first line."

Teacher: "And what number is right there at your peg?"

Student: "It's half... wait, half is one-quarter?"

Diagnosis: The student is experiencing Root 2: The whole is ignored (or assumed to be the full visual line rather than the unit distance from 0 to 1).

Root 1
Counting Parts Over Whole

Treats any cut as 'a half', unequal folds as quarters, or counts shaded boxes without verifying equal partition areas.

Root 2
The Whole is Ignored

Assumes 'half is always the same size' regardless of the whole; compares fractions from cakes of different sizes.

Root 3
Fraction as 2 Whole Numbers

Thinks 18 > 13 because 8 > 3; adds across: 15 + 25 = 310; reads '2 over 3'.

Root 4
Reasoning Only Whole → Part

Cannot reverse: given a half or 35, cannot construct the whole. Struggles to solve 1 − 13 without a formula.

Executive Professional Learning Synthesis

Key Pedagogical Takeaways (K–6 Continuum)

Linear Continuous Models First

Prioritise continuous paper ribbons, fraction strips, and number lines with one-directional folds before multi-directional area models. This grounds fractions firmly in division rather than counting discrete pieces.

The Whole Dictates the Size

Fractions can only be compared when wholes are equal. Emphasise that one-half of a 6 m plank is greater than two-thirds of a 3 m plank. On number lines, 1 must always be identified first.

Fraction Language Precision

Consistently say "two-thirds", never "2 over 3" or "2 out of 3". Teach fraction families using the Latin denominator (namer) and numerator (counter). Reserve symbols for Stage 2 and beyond.

Reversible & Relational Reasoning

Continually challenge students to rebuild the whole from a given part (e.g. "if this is a quarter, build the strip", or "if 35 is 18, find the whole"). Reversible reasoning builds genuine multiplicative coordination.

Next Steps for Stage Collaboration Teams
1. Classroom Quick Check Administer one clinical interview check (e.g. positioning 23 on a 0–2 number line) with 3 diverse learners.
2. Evidence Sharing Bring 2 student work samples to your next stage planning meeting to classify the root misconception together.
3. Consistent Tool Usage Adopt one shared visual representation (e.g. tape diagrams or number line fraction rulers) across your entire stage.
