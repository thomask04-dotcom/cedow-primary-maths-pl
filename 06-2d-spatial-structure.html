The Vertical Trajectory & Big Ideas

Syllabus outcomes, Working Mathematically connections, and developmental progressions from Early Stage 1 to Stage 3.

ES1 • Kindergarten Perceptual Foundations & Direct Comparison
MAE-2DS-01 MAE-2DS-02

2D Shapes Focus

Sort, describe, name, and make familiar shapes (triangles, circles, squares, rectangles). Explore lines and curves. Manipulate and turn shapes to fit into a space (Reasons about spatial relations).

NESA Teaching Advice Alert: Contrast prototypical equilateral triangles with non-examples (chevrons, kites, open or curved figures) so students do not misidentify right-angled triangles as “half a triangle”.

Area Focus

Identify area as the amount of surface inside a closed shape (“the inside part”). Compare areas directly using drawing, tracing, superimposing (placing one on top), or superposing (aligning corners and edges).

Working Mathematically (MAO-WM-01): Predict which of two surfaces will have the larger area and justify reasoning before direct physical verification.
Stage 1 • Years 1–2 Features, Turns & Informal Array Tiling
MA1-2DS-01 MA1-2DS-02

2D Shapes Focus

Describe polygons by sides and vertices. Recognize shapes in any orientation. Combine and split shapes (e.g. 3 triangles form a square, rectangle, or larger triangle). Quarter/half turns linked to clockface hands. Introduce slides (translations) and reflections without change in size.

Orientation Alert: A square turned onto its vertex is still a square, not a separate geometric species called a “diamond”.

Area Focus

Measure area using uniform informal units in rows and columns without gaps or overlaps. Progress from multiple tiles to a single square tile to construct the array structure. Explain why area remains constant when units are rearranged (e.g. 2 × 8 rectangle rearranged to 4 × 4 square).

NESA Advice (From Many Squares to One): Restricting students to a single square forces them to draw or visualise composite units (rows/columns), creating the cognitive bridge to multiplication.
Stage 2 • Years 3–4 Properties, Tessellation & Standard Metric Units
MA2-2DS-01 MA2-2DS-02 MA2-2DS-03

2D Shapes Focus

Compare quadrilaterals: parallelograms, rhombuses, rectangles, squares, trapeziums, kites. Identify parallel sides and right angles. Investigate symmetry in Aboriginal artworks and Islamic designs. Tessellate scalene triangles to form parallelograms (showing any triangle tessellates).

Terminology Shift: Strictly transition from “slide, flip, turn” to “translate, reflect, rotate”. Use “rhombus” instead of “diamond”.

Area Focus

Measure area in square centimetres (cm2) and square metres (m2) using grid overlays. Measure right-angled triangles by halving bounding rectangles. Establish that one square metre need not be a square (e.g. 2 m × 0.5 m).

Language Note: Record units in words (“square centimetres” / “sq m”). Formal exponential notation (cm2) is consolidated in Stage 3.
Stage 3 • Years 5–6 Overlapping Hierarchies, Large Units & Transformational Area
MA3-2DS-01 MA3-2DS-02 MA3-2DS-03

2D Shapes Focus

Classify triangles (equilateral, isosceles, scalene) and quadrilaterals based on side and angle properties. Recognize overlapping classes (e.g. a triangle can be both right-angled and isosceles; a square is a regular rhombus and rectangle). Investigate line and rotational symmetry.

Features vs Properties: A feature is directly observable (e.g. 3 sides); a property requires mathematical reasoning/deduction (e.g. angle sum is 180°).

Area Focus

Calculate areas of rectangles in words: length × width. Introduce hectares (ha) and square kilometres (km2). Dissect and transform composite L-shapes, parallelograms (base × height), and triangles (half base × height) into equivalent rectangles.

NESA Advice (Grid Behind the Shape): Place grids behind shapes rather than on top to deliberately compel multiplicative thinking over count-by-one strategies.

Section 2

Key Conceptual Shifts & Pedagogical Traps

Research-informed insights grounded in the NESA Teaching Advice, Clements & Sarama, Lowrie et al. (2021), and Hourigan & Leavy (2015).

1

Visual to Structural Reasoning

Students initially judge shapes holistically (“It looks like a roof, so it’s a triangle”). Progressing through van Hiele levels requires deconstructing shapes into components (sides, vertices, angles) and eventually deducing relationships (e.g. why opposite sides of a parallelogram must be equal).

2

Units Without Gaps to Composite Arrays

Covering area begins with discovering that round counters leave gaps, whereas square tiles tessellate. The critical cognitive milestone is coordinating rows and columns as composite units rather than counting square units one by one like buttons on a table.

3

The Conservation of Area under Dissection

Transformations (translations, rotations, reflections) change orientation and position but never alter surface area. Stage 3 area calculations for triangles, parallelograms, and L-shapes rely entirely on dissecting shapes and rearranging them into equivalent rectangles.

Critical Classroom Traps & Research-Backed Interventions

The Research (Hourigan & Leavy 2015): Early exposure restricted to upright equilateral triangles creates rigid prototype schemas. When presented with a right-angled triangle or long scalene triangle, young students frequently state “It is half a triangle” because it lacks symmetry.

The Fix: Provide non-examples (chevrons, kites, shapes with curved edges) alongside scalene, acute, obtuse, and right-angled triangles in varied orientations. Direct attention to the defining features: three straight sides, three vertices, completely closed.

The Problem: When a cardboard square is balanced on a vertex, students often declare it is “no longer a square; it turned into a diamond”.

The Fix: Rotate the physical shape while students observe. Ask: Did the lengths of the sides change? Did the 4 right angles change? Explain that “diamond” is a colloquial gem term, not a geometric name. A square in any orientation remains a square, and all squares are also special rhombuses.

The Research (De Bock et al.): Students intuitively apply linear 1D proportionality to 2D space, assuming that doubling the side lengths of a rectangle doubles its area. Furthermore, students frequently assume rectangles with the same perimeter must have the same area.

The Fix: Model on grid paper: compare a 2 × 3 rectangle (area 6) with a 4 × 6 rectangle (area 24 = 4 times as large). Conduct the classic NESA perimeter investigation: construct all whole-number rectangles with a fixed perimeter of 20 cm to reveal areas ranging from 9 cm2 (1 × 9) up to 25 cm2 (5 × 5 square).

Diocesan PL Insight: When students read 4 cm2 aloud as “four centimetres squared”, they visualize a 4 cm × 4 cm square (which actually has an area of 16 cm2).

The Fix: Strictly model reading cm2 and m2 as “square centimetres” and “square metres”. 4 cm2 means four 1-cm square units, whereas “4 cm squared” is an operation: (4 cm)2 = 16 cm2.

Section 4

Student Diagnostic Clinic & Root Misconceptions

Diagnose student thinking across the K–6 continuum using the 4 core conceptual roots identified in research and diocesan data.

Root 1: Prototype Bias

Shapes judged by rigid mental prototypes or non-standard orientation rather than geometric attributes.

Root 2: Unstructured Units

Area measured with non-tessellating units (circles), leaving gaps/overlaps, or counting grid squares one by one.

Root 3: Area vs Length/Perimeter

Tracing boundaries instead of surface; assuming equal perimeter = equal area; the 1D linearity problem.

Root 4: Non-Conservation

Belief that rotating, sliding, reflecting, or dissecting a shape alters its intrinsic surface area.

Key Pedagogical Takeaways (K–6 Continuum)

2D Shapes & Geometric Reasoning
  • Prototypes to Attributes: Systematically use non-examples to prevent the equilateral triangle and upright square traps.
  • Precise Terminology: Eliminate “diamond” in favor of “rhombus”; transition from “slide/flip/turn” to “translate/reflect/rotate”.
  • Overlapping Classes: Guide Stage 3 students to see that geometric families overlap (a square is both a rectangle and a rhombus).
Area & Spatial Measurement
  • From Many to One: Move from multi-tile coverings to a single square tile to force visualization of composite rows and columns.
  • Grid Positioning: Place grid overlays behind rectangles in Stage 3 to compel multiplicative dimension strategies over counting ones.
  • Transformational Area: Base all triangle, parallelogram, and composite area on dissecting and rearranging into equivalent rectangles.
School Team Action: Share the Stage 2/3 paper-folding and tangram dissection activities at your next stage meeting to establish common Diocesan mathematical vocabulary.
